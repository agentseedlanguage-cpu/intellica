# Intellica Training — learning design evidence and implementation standard

**Review date:** 25 November 2026  
**Purpose:** Translate established cognitive and educational research into a practical, measurable learning system for the Architecture and Cybersecurity Academies.

## Design principle

A professional course should not equate exposure to content with competence. Learners need to retrieve knowledge, inspect expert performance, practise increasingly independent tasks, receive specific feedback, and transfer their learning to a new context.

The learning system should therefore combine:
- clear outcomes and diagnostic entry checks;
- concise explanations and worked examples;
- guided practice with gradually faded support;
- retrieval questions and distributed review;
- interleaved scenarios where learners must distinguish similar problems;
- model solutions and actionable rubrics;
- cumulative work products and realistic capstone tasks;
- feedback that tells the learner what to improve and how to improve it.

## Selected evidence

| Research | Evidence and limits | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Test-Enhanced Learning** | Experimental work found that retrieval testing improved delayed retention compared with repeated study in the tested conditions. | End lessons with recall questions that require retrieval before displaying explanations. Use questions as learning events, not just scores. |
| **Cepeda et al. (2006), Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks** | Meta-analysis of 317 experiments and 839 assessments; spacing and retention interval jointly influence later recall. | Revisit important concepts over time rather than relying on one end-of-course cram session. Review intervals should be configurable and evaluated. |
| **Kirschner et al. (2026), retrieval/distributed/interleaved practice meta-analysis** | Three-level meta-analysis of 53 classroom studies and 7,303 learners: average effects reported for retrieval (g=0.37), spacing (g=0.20) and interleaving (g=0.40). Effects varied with task, knowledge type, test format and feedback. The sample is school classrooms, not adult professional training. | Use these techniques contextually. Do not transfer classroom effect sizes directly to adult learners or promise a particular score gain. |
| **Dunlosky et al. (2013), Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques** | Review rated practice testing and distributed practice high utility across many conditions; summarisation, highlighting and rereading alone had lower utility. | Make retrieval and spaced practice central; use summaries as orientation, not as the main learning mechanism. |
| **Renkl (2013/2014), Toward an Instructionally Oriented Theory of Example-Based Learning** | Integrates research on worked examples, observational learning and analogical reasoning; examples are effective for initial cognitive-skill acquisition. | Show a complete worked solution and explain the reasoning, then fade scaffolding and require independent work. |
| **Freeman et al. (2014), Active Learning in STEM** | Meta-analysis of 225 undergraduate STEM studies found improved examination performance under active learning compared with traditional lecturing. | Pair exposition with applied decision-making, explanation, experiments and labs. This is evidence from undergraduate STEM, not a direct guarantee for every professional course. |
| **Hattie & Timperley (2007), The Power of Feedback** | Review shows feedback can have positive or negative effects depending on its type and delivery. | Feedback must be specific, task-focused and actionable. Avoid generic praise and feedback that gives a score without a next step. |
| **Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), Deliberate Practice** | Theoretical and empirical account of structured practice designed to improve performance. Later literature debates how broadly the framework generalises and how practice should be operationalised. | Design targeted exercises around known skill gaps, with informative feedback and repeat attempts. Do not equate seat time or repetitions alone with expertise. |

## Recommended lesson pattern

1. **Orient:** state the professional outcome, why it matters and the prerequisite knowledge.
2. **Diagnose:** ask one or two questions or use a short task to reveal misconceptions.
3. **Explain:** teach the key model, terminology and decision rules in short, accessible segments.
4. **Demonstrate:** show a worked example and make expert reasoning visible.
5. **Guide:** ask the learner to complete a related task with prompts, hints or partial scaffolds.
6. **Practise independently:** require a realistic task with constraints and observable output.
7. **Retrieve:** ask low-stakes questions without showing the answer first; provide explanations after the attempt.
8. **Review:** resurface critical concepts after a delay and interleave them with related scenarios.
9. **Reflect:** use a rubric and model answer to identify one concrete improvement.
10. **Transfer:** apply the same principles in a changed scenario or capstone, not merely a repeated example.

## Professional learning artefacts

Every open course should produce an artefact that resembles real work. Examples:

- architecture description, quality scenario, decision record, interface contract or risk register;
- agent threat model, tool-permission matrix, policy test, RAG provenance report or assurance dossier;
- incident timeline, detection rule, containment plan, vulnerability report or remediation retest.

A completeness checker must never be described as an assessor of correctness. Self-review is valuable, but it is not equivalent to independent marking.

## Assessment architecture

- Keep placement, formative practice and summative assessment separate.
- Blueprint every summative item to an outcome, cognitive demand, difficulty, risk and source.
- Use original items; never reproduce confidential external exam questions.
- Require application, analysis and judgement, not only definition recall.
- Use practical stations or capstones where performance is the intended outcome.
- Calibrate assessors, double-mark consequential practical work and publish appeals rules before credential launch.
- Track item exposure, difficulty, discrimination, distractor performance, reliability and fairness.
- Do not let a high knowledge score compensate for failure of a critical safety or security control.

## Evaluation and continuous improvement

Measure learning rather than activity alone:

- pre/post performance against the same competency blueprint;
- delayed retention at 30 and 60 days;
- transfer to a new problem or environment;
- quality of submitted artefacts against published rubrics;
- completion and retry patterns by lesson;
- item difficulty, discrimination, distractor function and exposure;
- practical task success, safety failures and control bypasses;
- learner accessibility, device and bandwidth barriers;
- employer/mentor feedback on workplace transfer.

Do not claim the method is “the best” based on design intent. Validate it through learner studies, item analysis, independent review and observed transfer.

## Accessibility and delivery requirements

- transcripts and captions for all instructional media;
- keyboard access, visible focus and screen-reader labels;
- readable typography, contrast and responsive layouts;
- short segments with save/resume support;
- downloadable text/reference materials where licensing permits;
- low-bandwidth and mobile-friendly lab alternatives;
- synthetic data and local-only practice for sensitive or offensive security scenarios;
- clear accommodations and assessment adjustment procedures.
